## 01  A LETTER TO THE TEAM

Why I built Napkin

My story with Napkin does not begin with AI. It begins with elBulli.

I spent more than twenty years in advertising. I became completely absorbed by creative work: ideas, art direction, craft and awards. I loved it. I still love it. But eventually I reached a point where I had to ask myself a harder question: what is all this for? I needed the work to have deeper meaning.

I stepped away for several reasons. I wanted to find a way back that could make me better and, in some small way, make the industry better too. I do not believe meaningful change usually arrives in one grand gesture. It happens through tiny percentages, preserved and compounded over time.

That is why I kept returning to elBulli. Ferran Adrià and his team treated creativity as a discipline. They built the first gastronomic research and development workshop and organised the restaurant around a radical cycle: six months open, six months closed for research and development. They did not protect the present at the expense of the future. They created space to experiment, document, learn and return stronger.

Napkin is built on the same conviction. Client work matters. But we must also protect time for invention. We need a studio, a laboratory and a memory. We need to study how ideas happen, build tools that help people make better work and share what we learn.

For the first months, I was doing this largely by myself. It was exciting, but it could also feel isolating. Then Sai and Shrey arrived. Now Vahid is joining us. I cannot properly describe the joy of having a team around the work. Working with a team is what I do best. It is where I find my energy.

You can walk a long way alone. With the right people, you can run.

I want you to know where this came from because the next chapter belongs to all of us. Nobody knows exactly where it goes. That is the point. We will find out together.

## 06  THE CIRCLE

The mark is a gesture and an operating system

Napkin begins with the oldest complete shape and gives it a new job.

In Japanese Zen art, the ensō is a circle made as a direct brush gesture. It is associated with meditation, awakening, emptiness and wholeness. Its force comes from presence: the hand cannot hide behind polish, and the surrounding blank space is part of the work.

Our interpretation

Napkin does not use the ensō as ornament or claim a religious symbol as its own. We recognise a shared principle: a simple form can hold complexity without becoming complicated. Our circle is precise enough to guide a system and alive enough to carry human judgement.

○ = portal + identity + decision + state + return

The O opens into the shared space. Circles choose; rectangles create. The sphere gives an agent personality; the circle gives the human control; the outer arc shows operational state. Every loop returns approved work, evidence and learning to the system.

One circular identity. One connected system. One continuous journey.

## 13  THE EVIDENCE

Why tools need an operating system

AI can improve knowledge work, but the benefit is uneven

A preregistered experiment involving 758 consultants found that, on eighteen tasks inside the capability frontier, people using GPT-4 completed 12.2 percent more tasks and worked 25.1 percent faster, with significantly better quality. On a deliberately difficult task outside the frontier, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer. This supports task routing, human judgement and verification rather than unrestricted use.

Parallel agents can outperform one general agent on the right work

Anthropic reports that its orchestrator-worker research system, using a lead agent and parallel specialist subagents, outperformed a single-agent configuration by 90.2 percent on an internal research evaluation. Parallelization cut research time by up to 90 percent on complex queries. These are vendor-reported internal results, not independent evidence, and they apply mainly to breadth-first research tasks.

Many AI projects fail for organizational reasons

RAND interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers. The study cites external estimates that more than 80 percent of AI projects fail, but the more useful finding is why: unclear problems, poor fit with business workflow and context, insufficient data, weak infrastructure, chasing new technology and attempting tasks beyond AI capability. RAND explicitly recommends focusing on enduring problems, infrastructure, domain context and technical limits.

The implication for Napkin

The model is not the system. The advantage has to come from problem definition, context, orchestration, specialist roles, evaluation, approvals, infrastructure and memory. A licence gives the organization intelligence. Napkin must turn that intelligence into accountable creative capability.

## 16  THE LEARNING ENGINE

Do not optimise prompts  Optimise loops

PLAN  →  EXECUTE  →  REVIEW  →  REMEMBER  →  REPEAT

Every project should leave the system stronger than it found it. Napkin captures what was approved, why it was approved, what failed, what it cost and how it was produced. That context becomes available to the next team and the next agent.

The compounding model

Cₙ = C₀ × (1 + g)ⁿ

C is retained capability, g is the measurable gain preserved after each cycle and n is the number of completed cycles. This is a model for measurement, not a promise. If learning is not captured, verified and reused, it does not compound.

What we measure

Time from brief to approved work

Number and quality of viable territories explored

Human review time and rework

Cost per approved output

Reuse of approved knowledge and assets

Brand accuracy, originality and production quality

## 17  THE PARALLELISM MODEL

How Napkin changes agency economics

The illustration holds the work constant: six four-hour workstreams plus four hours of handovers take 28 elapsed hours in a serial chain. If the longest stream takes four hours, orchestration takes 1.5 hours and human review takes three, the parallel path takes 8.5 hours. That is 3.29× elapsed-time compression in this scenario, not a universal claim.

COST PER APPROVED OUTCOME = (HUMAN + MODEL + COMPUTE + QA) ÷ APPROVED OUTPUTS

The CFO test is simple: faster work must survive review. Measure cycle time, approval yield, human attention, rework and reuse. The scarce resource is trusted judgement.
